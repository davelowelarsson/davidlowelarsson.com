During Preparedness Week in September 2026, or Beredskapsveckan here in Sweden,
I ran our second Game Day. We had done this before, it was really fun and people
learned a lot, so I wanted to do it again. The main reason was still the same...
get some practice handling incidents before we have to do it for real.

<MediaAside
  src={gameDayLogo}

  caption="The logo I made for the event."
>

Two mixed teams had their own production-like exercise environments, with
production kept outside the exercise. Colleagues played users and the business,
and I had six challenges prepared for the morning.

Colleagues outside our team were working through a tabletop scenario that week,
and we decided to combine them. Not just practising the theoretical side... but
also the practical, on systems that actually broke, while answering questions
from everyone else.

</MediaAside>

## Before the morning

The biggest thing for me was probably the preparation. Making half a day
successful and fun took roughly three to six times as long in preparation. Breaking
something sounds fairly simple... but I needed to know what the teams would
see and how to put it back.

So I rehearsed the faults, checked the symptoms, checked the recovery, and went
back over it again, and again, and again... until it worked as expected. One
planned outage initially left the application serving requests, because the old
version kept running while its broken replacement failed to start. Good to find
out before two teams and a timer were in front of it.

So I wrote a Game Day shell script to inject and revert the faults and send
messages to the two Slack channels (each team had one, where everything was
communicated). Starting a challenge injected the fault, announced it in Slack if
it was meant to be announced, and pushed an update to the Grafana dashboard.
Then I could stop the challenge with the same script... and then start the next
one. The script made everything very reproducible and helped a lot to do this
systematically.

There were cue cards for the colleagues playing stakeholders, and hints
prepared as questions. My own notes included a reminder to resist helping when
a team was stuck (a little easier to write down than to do, I think).

## The challenges

We started with a briefing about why we were doing this, followed by a first
episode with three challenges. After a fifteen-minute break came a second
episode with three more, called "Is it an attack?", and then a debrief. So more
like four parts to the morning.

</MediaAside>

Some faults were obvious, others didn't turn anything red:

| Challenge | Change in the exercise environment | Visible effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Service outage | Made a service fail to start | Errors and repeated restarts |
| Search failure | Interrupted a dependency connection | Search hung while the rest of the site worked |
| Incomplete data | Removed part of the result data | Results arrived without images |
| App access | Changed an exercise credential | Apps were denied access while the web still worked |
| Unfamiliar workload | Added background work that didn't belong | No obvious outage |
| Publishing failure | Broke a database connection | Pages responded but content was stale |

The dashboard gave each team its own column. Scoring depended on them explaining
what they thought was wrong, what they had done and how they had checked it,
which also left us something to go back through afterwards.

## In the room

Colleagues from UX and product reported what they could see and asked for a
change before a deadline. Someone describing missing images could be precise
about what was wrong on screen without knowing the system behind it, and the
teams needed to answer while they were still investigating.

Partway through, our manager walked into the rooms carrying a printed ransom
note. It was marked as an exercise, and our manager was also meant to take
focus... and intentionally obstruct the work already going on. The teams had to
work out who else needed to know while being interrupted.

In the debrief we talked about the stress. The messages in Slack got shorter,
communication in general was affected, and requests from the business still
needed an answer while people were investigating.

</MediaAside>

The AI tools were very helpful... but they also took time and attention from
what was going on. An AI-assisted triage took at least a minute or two, just to
get an idea of what was wrong, and getting an exact root cause was sometimes
possible and sometimes not (I had hidden some of them too deep even for our AI
triage skill). So everyone investigating had to weigh the time it took to use AI
against checking themselves, on a challenge that maybe had 13 minutes from start
to finish.

## After the exercise

I had paper worksheets too... but actually most of what we learned was
documented directly in the Slack channels. Only one person used the paper, and
wrote something down around the third challenge, no one else. The Slack channels
were used much more.

This was a definite learning. Some people playing stakeholders also took roles
inside a team, two jobs at once, so I think the next Game Day would need a
clearer separation of responsibilities... or more of a blue vs red team, or
automating each episode completely, with stakeholder messages and other "stress
signals" included. But it also pulled product owners, UX and even managers into
the exercise, increasing the stress while getting them engaged. No one was
bored 😅 which could have been a risk if they hadn't leaned in.

A product owner even said she felt how uncomfortable it was to ask for a
delivery while knowing the team was already busy solving the incident. That's
not uncommon in the real world, and a good, important lesson.

But we also aimed for it to be an ordinary day... people have meetings, get sick
or have important doctor's appointments. That's just how the real world is, and
also something we need to practise and experience.

So I'm undecided here... it served a good purpose, we'll see how the next one
turns out.
