During Preparedness Week in September 2026, or Beredskapsveckan here in Sweden,
I ran our second Game Day. We had done this before, it was really fun and people
learned a lot, so I wanted to do it again. The main reason was still the same...
get some practice handling incidents before we have to do it for real.

Two mixed teams had their own production-like exercise environments, with
production kept outside the exercise. Colleagues played users and the business,
and I had six challenges prepared for the morning.

The same week, colleagues were working through a tabletop scenario about the
decisions around an incident. Our exercise brought in the practical side, with
systems that actually broke and people who had to investigate while answering
questions from everyone else.

</MediaAside>

## Before the morning

The biggest thing for me was probably the amount of preparation. To make half a
day successful and fun took roughly three to six times as long in preparation,
and quite a lot of that was checking whether things actually worked the way I
thought they would. Breaking something sounds fairly simple... but I also
needed to know what the teams would see and how to put it back.

So I rehearsed the faults, checked the symptoms, checked the recovery, and went
back over the ones that behaved differently from what I expected. One planned
outage initially left the application serving requests, because the old version
kept running while its broken replacement failed to start. That was useful to
learn before putting two teams in front of it and starting a timer.

There were cue cards for the colleagues playing stakeholders, hints prepared as
questions, and a running order for me to follow. My own notes included a reminder
to resist helping when a team was stuck (a little easier to write down than to
do, I think).

## The challenges

I split the morning into two parts, with a short debrief and a break between
them. The faults ranged from an obvious outage to something unfamiliar running
without breaking anything, so the teams couldn't rely on every challenge
announcing itself with a red dashboard. These are the scenarios, with the
systems kept anonymous:

| Challenge | Change in the exercise environment | Visible effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Service outage | Made a service fail to start | Errors and repeated restarts |
| Search failure | Interrupted a dependency connection | Search hung while the rest of the site worked |
| Incomplete data | Removed part of the result data | Results arrived without images |
| App access | Changed an exercise credential | Apps were denied access while the web still worked |
| Unfamiliar workload | Added background work that didn't belong | No obvious outage |
| Publishing failure | Broke a database connection | Pages responded but content was stale |

The dashboard gave each team its own column, and I had controls for starting
faults and recording points alongside the live signals. Scoring depended on the
teams explaining what they thought was wrong, what they had done and how they
had checked it. That also left us something to go back through afterwards.

## In the room

Colleagues from UX and product reported what they could see and asked for a
change before a deadline. Someone describing missing images could be precise
about what was wrong on screen without knowing the system behind it, and the
teams needed to answer while they were still investigating.

Partway through, our manager walked into the rooms carrying a printed ransom
note. It was marked as an exercise, but it arrived while other things were
already competing for attention. The security scenario gave us another question
to work through... who else needed to know, and which decisions belonged outside
the team?

The stress came up in the debrief too. Everyone knew it was an exercise, but the
messages got shorter as the morning went on, and the requests from the business
still needed an answer while people were trying to understand a fault.

</MediaAside>

AI-assisted tooling was allowed and used during the investigation, with the
same expectation that findings needed checking. The notes also contained a
successful repair with an explanation that didn't match the fault I had
introduced. Getting something working again gave us a result, but we still
needed to check our explanation before carrying it into the incident write-up.

## After the exercise

We used paper worksheets to separate facts from assumptions, alongside actions
and communication. One recorded a careful observation and a hypothesis that
turned out to be wrong, which meant we could look at how reasonable it had
seemed at the time. The debrief also brought up how people found their way from
a symptom to the tools they needed.

There were things in how I organised it that needed revisiting too. Some people
playing stakeholders also took roles inside a team, giving them two jobs at
once, and changed attendance meant the teams weren't evenly matched. Hybrid
participation needed more attention as well, so I couldn't read the scoreboard
as a fair comparison of how the teams worked.

I built a retrospective into the follow-up, using the notes and worksheets
alongside a timeline of when faults began, when signals appeared and when people
responded. I wanted us to agree on a few improvements together, with someone
responsible for each, instead of arriving with all the conclusions already made.
