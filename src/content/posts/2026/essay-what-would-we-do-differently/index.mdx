I got a nice forecast running at work for total stream starts, I was quite happy with it.
The error rate looked impressive... but then came the question, how does this help .. in practice?

Our precogs, nod to *Minority Report*, were mostly
seeing our service's rhythm, it was kind of satisfying, but I was wondering how to get it even better.

All numbers are mostly obfuscated to protect sensitive data. But the error rates are still the same as when I ran the experiment.

## TL;DR

- TimesFM with a calendar reached 6.9% average daily percentage error in tests on past data, beating simple baselines.
- The total hid the title-level changes a campaign budget might act on, and those smaller forecasts were harder.
- Campaign counts didn't improve the total forecast. Releases and campaigns overlapped, so the title-level comparisons couldn't isolate what the ads did.
- The calendar and honest scoring took more work than running the model. Next time I'll start with the decision.

## What we could predict

I gave Google's [TimesFM 2.5](https://github.com/google-research/timesfm) daily stream-start
history without retraining it. It was already trained on other time series and ran locally in seconds (it was impressively fast).

We added a Swedish calendar as *covariates*, a word I learned doing this.
They're extra inputs alongside the stream-history, with dates we know ahead of time. We used public holidays,
bridge days and separate flags for the big holidays, such as Christmas and Midsummer.

School breaks were weighted by pupil numbers, using real dates for the largest municipalities
and estimated dates for the rest. The calendar model learned from our viewing history, while TimesFM itself wasn't retrained.

The *ensemble* combines TimesFM with the calendar model, leaning on the calendar on holidays.
We compared it with repeating last week's viewing and using last year adjusted for growth.

In tests on past data, our seven-day forecasts were 6.9% away from actual daily viewing on average.
We used the same years to improve the calendar and had less than a week of live tracking then.

Even on weekdays the error was less than half last year's. Easter moves between years, so the
baseline can compare a holiday with an ordinary day, but Midsummer was where last year won.
There aren't many Christmas Eves or Midsummers to learn from...

Our first Easter-break rule put breaks a week late. Local dates brought one calendar backtest's
error from about 30% to about 14%. Better calendar data did more than fiddling with the model.

Across our tests, the forecast range was supposed to contain the actual result about eight times out of ten. That held up on ordinary weekdays, weekends and public holidays, but on big holidays and school breaks it was closer to seven.

We saved our live forecasts before seeing the results and kept those separate from tests on old data. An artificial intelligence (AI) review caught mistakes in how we were testing that could make the forecasts look better than they really were. The model was the easy part, not fooling ourselves was the work.

## What would an ad change?

So I thought a marketer might have a useful question for it: where should we put money to get
the most back from a campaign budget? Facebook/Meta, YouTube or television, and would extra spending bring extra viewing?

But we only counted campaigns of different sizes. We didn't test channels or spending,
so this couldn't answer the budget question.
With longer history, adding counts changed error from 7.8% to 7.9%, and with shorter history
from 9.3% to 10.7%, which surprised me.

The total adds together lots of different viewing, which makes it easier to predict and hides
the title-level changes I'd want to act on. Several campaigns could run at once and recur each year,
so some of their pattern might already be in the history.

In a small title-level test, error was around 21% using weighted absolute percentage error (WAPE),
which weights errors by viewing volume.
The total's 6.9% uses MAPE, an average of daily percentage errors, so this isn't a direct
three-times-worse comparison. Those smaller forecasts were harder, but closer to the decision.

During campaigns with recent or new episodes, median daily viewing roughly doubled compared with
the four weeks before. Without new episodes it fell about 20%. On the same five titles, the release
bump with a campaign was about 0.9 times the bump without one. That looked more like big releases
getting campaigns than ads creating the bump, but five titles is far from proof.

I think forecasting is very cool, but without a real use case I'm not sure what I'd use it for.
I built the forecast first and went looking for a decision afterwards...

So we got our "20/20 hindsight", which is a bit funny for an experiment with precogs.
What should we ask them to predict, and what would we do differently with the answer?
I don't know yet 😄
